# Great Salt Lake Shorelines: page text

All of the explanatory text for the interactive, kept here so it can be reused in a caption, post, handout, or README. Numbers are the values computed from the current data (fall 1985–2026; no outline for 2025), measured against the lake's historic (long-term) average level of 4,207 feet. That is not the recent average: the falls of 1997–2026 averaged 4,194.8 feet, about 12 feet lower.

## Title block

**Eyebrow:** Seen from space · every fall, 1985–2026

**Title:** Great Salt Lake Shorelines

**Introduction**

Each outline is Great Salt Lake's shoreline one fall, traced from satellite images and stacked by year from 1985 at the bottom to 2026 at the top. Orange means the lake was below 4,207 feet, its historic average level; blue means it was above. It has been below its historic average every fall since 1988. At the 2022 low it was 18.1 feet below the historic average, leaving about 1,230 square miles of lakebed dry that is underwater at that level, and bringing it back up would have taken about 5.5 trillion gallons of water, enough to fill 8.3 million Olympic swimming pools.

## How this was made

Each outline is the lake's typical shoreline between September 1 and November 15 that year, traced from every cloud-free Landsat satellite image in that window. Landsat sees the ground in 30-meter squares, about the size of a baseball infield. Dashed outlines and hollow dots mark years when clouds hid more than a tenth of the shoreline, so read those with some caution.

At its historic average of 4,207 feet, the lake covers about 2,208 square miles on these satellite outlines, based on the 3 years the lake sat within 3 feet of that level (1985, 1987, and 1988). USGS lakebed maps put it a little smaller, about 2,125 square miles; satellites count wet mud at the water's edge, so their outlines run about 4% larger. "Dry lakebed" is that size minus the lake's size that fall. Lake level is the average from September 1 to November 15 at the USGS gauge at Saltair Boat Harbor on the south arm (station 10010000); 2026 runs through November 4.

Water needed is the volume between that fall's lake level and 4,207 feet. It comes from U.S. Geological Survey maps of the lakebed, which give the lake's size at every level, added up from one level to the next. It assumes the whole lake rises together, measured at the south-arm gauge near Saltair. The north arm usually sits a little lower, so the real amount is somewhat larger. As a check on the method, rising from 4,191 to 4,198 feet comes to about 5.2 million acre-feet, close to published estimates of about 5 million. One acre-foot (325,851 gallons) covers a football field, end zones included, about 9 inches deep; an Olympic pool holds about 660,000 gallons.

When you pick a year, its shoreline drops (or rises) to that fall's lake level. The gray outline shows where the shore sits at the 4,207-foot historic average, and the shaded wall between them is the missing water. Heights are stretched so the difference is easy to see: 1 foot of lake level takes the space of 1 year in the stack.

## Repairs and limits

A railroad causeway crosses the lake and splits it into north and south arms. The drawing joins the two arms across it, so the causeway doesn't show up as a straight line through the water. In 2012 a failed part on the Landsat 7 satellite left striped gaps across the north arm, and in 2017 the satellite images missed the west half of that arm. Those missing pieces are filled in from the year whose shoreline matched best where it could be seen (2005 for 2012, 2019 for 2017) and drawn dotted. Their sizes are estimates: about 1,303 square miles in 2012 (1,206 as the satellite saw it) and about 1,244 in 2017 (997 as seen). Thin seams between satellite images in 2024 are closed the same way.

Farmington Bay and Bear River Bay count as part of the lake only in years when they connect to it. In 1987 floodwater spread onto low ground west of the lake, past the edge of the area mapped, so that outline is cut off with a straight edge. That year is one of the three used to size the lake at 4,207 feet, so the lake size at the historic average may be slightly low.
