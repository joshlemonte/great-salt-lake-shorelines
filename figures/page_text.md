## Title block

**Eyebrow:** Seen from space · every fall since 1985

**Title:** Great Salt Lake Shorelines

**Introduction**

Each outline is Great Salt Lake's shoreline one fall, traced from satellite images and stacked by year from 1985 at the bottom to 2026 at the top. Orange means the lake was below 4,198 feet, the bottom of its healthy range; blue means it was above. It has been too low every fall since 2002. At the 2022 low it was 9.1 feet short, leaving about 502 square miles of lakebed dry, and refilling it would have taken about 2.1 trillion gallons of water, enough to fill 3.2 million Olympic swimming pools.

**Why 4,198 feet?**

The Utah Department of Natural Resources treats 4,198 to 4,205 feet above sea level as the lake's healthy range. Below it the water gets saltier, the brine shrimp and the migrating birds that feed on them come under stress, and dry lakebed sends dust toward nearby towns.

## How this was made

Each outline is the lake's typical shoreline between September 1 and November 15 that year, traced from every cloud-free Landsat satellite image in that window. Landsat sees the ground in 30-meter squares, about the size of a baseball infield. Dashed outlines and hollow dots mark years when clouds hid more than a tenth of the shoreline, so read those with some caution.

A healthy lake at 4,198 feet covers about 1,481 square miles on these satellite outlines, based on the 13 years the lake sat within 3 feet of that level. USGS lakebed maps put it a little smaller, about 1,422 square miles; satellites count wet mud at the water's edge, so their outlines run about 5% larger. "Lakebed left dry" is that healthy size minus the lake's size that fall. Lake level is the average from September 1 to November 15 at the USGS gauge at Saltair Boat Harbor on the south arm (station 10010000); 2026 runs through November 4.

Water needed is the volume between that fall's lake level and 4,198 feet. It comes from U.S. Geological Survey maps of the lakebed, which give the lake's size at every level, added up from one level to the next. It assumes the whole lake rises together, measured at the south-arm gauge near Saltair. The north arm usually sits a little lower, so the real amount is somewhat larger. As a check, rising from 4,191 feet comes to about 5.2 million acre-feet, close to published estimates of about 5 million. One acre-foot (325,851 gallons) covers a football field, end zones included, about 9 inches deep; an Olympic pool holds about 660,000 gallons.

When you pick a year, its shoreline drops (or rises) to that fall's lake level. The gray outline shows where the shore sits at a healthy 4,198 feet, and the shaded wall between them is the missing water. Heights are stretched so the difference is easy to see: 1 foot of lake level takes the space of 1 year in the stack.

## Repairs and limits

A railroad causeway crosses the lake and splits it into north and south arms. The drawing joins the two arms across it, so the causeway doesn't show up as a straight line through the water. In 2012 a failed part on the Landsat 7 satellite left striped gaps across the north arm, and in 2017 the satellite images missed the west half of that arm. Those missing pieces are filled in from the year whose shoreline matched best where it could be seen (2005 for 2012, 2019 for 2017) and drawn dotted. Their sizes are estimates: about 1,303 square miles in 2012 (1,206 as the satellite saw it) and about 1,244 in 2017 (997 as seen). Thin seams between satellite images in 2024 are closed the same way.

Farmington Bay and Bear River Bay count as part of the lake only in years when they connect to it. In 1987 floodwater spread onto low ground west of the lake, past the edge of the area mapped, so that outline is cut off with a straight edge.
